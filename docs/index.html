01The Threat

A water tower, increasingly on fire.

Mount Kenya supplies roughly 40% of the country's water and around 60% of its hydropower, feeding the Tana and Ewaso Nyiro rivers. Its forests are also increasingly flammable.

Wildfires here are not new; paleoecological evidence puts fire on the mountain for at least 26,000 years. What has changed is frequency and scale, driven by climate variability, illegal logging, and agricultural encroachment at the forest edge. The March 2012 fire, which is the focus of this study, burned for weeks through upper montane forest and bamboo. Less than a decade later, the February 2019 fire dwarfed it.

Existing recovery models lean almost entirely on vegetation indices like NDVI and NBR. This study asks what's missing: does Land Surface Temperature , a variable that governs evapotranspiration and soil moisture, help explain why some burned slopes green up again and others don't?

02Study Area

Five vegetation zones, one volcanic peak.

Map: Leaflet / CARTO dark basemap · rings approximate the elevation bands described in the study, centred on the summit (≈0.15°S, 37.31°E).

Rising to 5,199 m, Mount Kenya's vegetation is stratified by altitude, and each band burns and recovers differently.

  • Lower Montane Forest — 1,200–2,500 m. Borders farmland; biodiverse but heavily logged.
  • Bamboo Zone — 2,200–3,200 m. Dense Arundinaria alpina; fire-sensitive.
  • Upper Montane Forest — 2,500–3,500 m. Cedar-dominated; least fire-resilient.
  • Ericaceous Zone — 3,500–4,000 m. Fire-adapted shrubs; slow to resprout.
  • Alpine / Nival — above 4,000 m. Dwarf vegetation, rock, and glacier.

    What correlates with recovery

    Pearson r against NBR change (Fig. 13, correlation matrix)

    Burn severity (dNBR) and pre-fire NBR mattered most but soil total organic carbon and temperature variables outweighed LST itself in raw importance. Correlation, though, told a more direct story: warmer mean and maximum temperatures tracked positively with recovery, while higher soil carbon and higher elevation tracked negatively — a reminder that colder, high-carbon upper-montane sites recovered more slowly than warmer lower slopes.

    08The Temperature Sweet Spot

    Recovery peaks in a narrow thermal band.

    The partial dependence plot for Land Surface Temperature reveals a sharp, non-linear relationship with recovery.

    Below roughly 299 K, colder surfaces show a flat, negative effect on recovery, consistent with slower evapotranspiration and metabolic activity at higher, cooler elevations. Around 299–300 K, the effect spikes upward: a narrow thermal sweet spot where conditions favour regrowth. Push past ~301 K, and the effect turns volatile and declines again, which is a signal of heat and moisture stress working against recovery.

    The practical implication: post-fire management that moderates surface temperature i.e. mulching, shading, temperature-tolerant replanting, could nudge severely burned, exposed slopes back toward that recovery-favouring range.

    Reading the curve

    2012 — catastrophic canopy loss registers immediately.

    2013–2016 — slow stabilization near the baseline; recovery is real but faint.

    2017–2021 — a more visible, if uneven, positive trend, peaking in 2020, nine years after the fire that started it all.

    10What This Means

    Recovery models need a temperature term.

    Three management implications follow directly from the model's findings.

    Severity-specific response

    Triage by burn class

    Statistically distinct recovery trajectories across severity classes (Tukey HSD, p<.001) mean high-severity patches need earlier, more intensive intervention than lightly burned ones.

    Thermal management

    Keep surfaces in range

    Since recovery is most favoured near 299–300 K, mulching, shading, and staged replanting on exposed slopes could shift local conditions back toward that band.

    Elevation-aware planning

    Don't treat the mountain as one unit

    Higher, cooler, higher-carbon sites recover on a different clock than lower montane forest. Restoration budgets and timelines should reflect that gradient.

    More broadly, the study argues that Land Surface Temperature,long underused next to vegetation indices in post-fire recovery science, deserves a permanent place in the predictor set, particularly for tropical montane ecosystems like Mount Kenya where elevation, temperature, and moisture are so tightly coupled. Future work should extend this to species-specific recovery dynamics and longer, higher-resolution time series as more Landsat and Sentinel data accumulate.
